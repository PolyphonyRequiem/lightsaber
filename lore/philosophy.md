# Philosophy of the Force: Jedi, Sith, and the Traditions of Balance

The lightsaber is the visible answer to an invisible question. The crystal at its heart is shaped by the wielder, and the wielder is shaped by a tradition. This document treats the spiritual architecture of the Jedi Order, the Sith, and the traditions that orbit and oppose them. Disney-era canon and pre-2014 Legends material are distinguished where it matters.

## The Jedi Code

### The Modern Code

The five-line mantra recited by Jedi Knights and Masters in the late Republic era reads:

> There is no emotion, there is peace.
> There is no ignorance, there is knowledge.
> There is no passion, there is serenity.
> There is no chaos, there is harmony.
> There is no death, there is the Force.

This text is canonical. It is recited by Luke Skywalker as narrator in the canon reference *Secrets of the Jedi*, and appears in *Disciple* and other Clone Wars-era canon material (Wookieepedia, "Jedi Code," Mantra section). Its real-world origin is the 1987 *Star Wars: The Roleplaying Game* (West End Games), where it was paired with practical conduct rules that later fell away. The mantra survived; the conduct rules did not.

In-universe, the modern Code is "believed to have been written by Jedi Master Odan-Urr" (Wookieepedia "Jedi Code," History section, citing the canon sourcebook *Nexus of Power*, 2016). Odan-Urr is a semi-legendary figure in canon and a fully developed character in Legends. Claudia Gray's canon novel *Master and Apprentice* (2019) refers to the Code's author obliquely when Qui-Gon Jinn thinks "whoever wrote the Code never had to deal with the Hutts."

### Line by Line

**"There is no emotion, there is peace."** "Emotion" here means raw, reactive, destabilizing affect, not feeling as such. George Lucas was explicit: "the Jedi are trained, allowed and expected to love people (even their enemies, the Sith), but they are not to form attachments" (Wikipedia "Jedi," sourcing Rinzler's *Making of* volumes and the Taschen *Star Wars Archives*). Peace is the relational ground beneath reactivity, not the elimination of inner life.

**"There is no ignorance, there is knowledge."** The Jedi tradition is monastic and academic. Lucas in 2020 described the Jedi as "designed to be a Buddhist monk who happened to be very good at fighting." The line affirms that suffering arises from delusion and that disciplined attention can correct it.

**"There is no passion, there is serenity."** Passion differs from emotion. Passion is affect that has become directed and compulsive. Serenity is feeling that has structure without compulsion. The two distinct words negate two different things.

**"There is no chaos, there is harmony."** Beneath apparent disorder, an underlying arrangement holds. The Force as binding element, the same Force Obi-Wan describes in *A New Hope*: "It surrounds us, penetrates us, it binds the galaxy together."

**"There is no death, there is the Force."** Death is transformation, not ending. Living things release into the Cosmic Force at death, and the Cosmic Force is the wellspring from which the Living Force emanates. Yoda in *Return of the Jedi*: "Twilight is upon me, and soon night must fall. That is the way of things, the way of the Force."

### The Older Code

A second version of the mantra exists in canon, recited by Jedi younglings during their Initiate Trials and by Depa Billaba in *Kanan* #7 (Marvel, 2015):

> Emotion, yet peace.
> Ignorance, yet knowledge.
> Passion, yet serenity.
> Chaos, yet harmony.
> Death, yet the Force.

The structural difference is fundamental. The modern Code denies. The older Code acknowledges. The word "yet" performs a paradox rather than a negation. The initiate is invited to hold both realities at once. The youngling who recites this version is taught from the start that they have emotions and that peace is the ground of being, and that learning Jedi life means working out how both can be true.

Wookieepedia notes a third, even older version "considered old by 382 BBY" containing lines like "A Jedi does not act for personal power or wealth / But only seeks knowledge and enlightenment." The trajectory is suggestive. The oldest text was a statement of purpose. The middle text was a koan. The latest became a commandment. The Order grew more rigid as it aged.

### Code as Koan, Code as Commandment

The koan reading invites meditation. The practitioner sits with the paradox until experience produces understanding that rule-following could not. The commandment reading skips this and asserts that emotion has no claim on the practitioner. The commandment is easier to memorize and easier to institutionalize, but it generates a brittle and repressive system. A Jedi who genuinely feels grief, love, or fear is told their experience does not exist.

The High Republic Jedi (~500-100 BBY) sit closer to the koan reading. Jedi Master Indeera Stokes (canon, *The Rising Storm*) teaches that "love, experiencing and embracing joy, affection and even grief was part of the light side, however, a Jedi couldn't be a slave of these emotions." The Prequel-era Jedi (~100-19 BBY) sit closer to the commandment reading.Yoda counsels Anakin in *Revenge of the Sith* to "rejoice" at the prospect of Padme's death and to "train yourself to let go of everything you fear to lose." The advice is technically correct doctrine and emotionally catastrophic. Anakin experiences it as a denial of his humanity. Lucas stated directly: "Anakin became Darth Vader because he didn't want [Padme] to die." Attachment was processed as sin rather than as a condition to be worked through.

Qui-Gon Jinn embodies the dissent within the Prequel Order. *Secrets of the Jedi* (canon) describes him as "better known for his efforts to follow the will of the Force than the tenets of the Code." Obi-Wan believed his master "would have served on the High Council if he had followed the Code." This is the canonical articulation of ossification. The Code as administered had become, for some, a constraint against the Force rather than an expression of it. Claudia Gray's *Master and Apprentice* dramatizes this through the lineage Yoda taught Dooku, Dooku taught Qui-Gon, Qui-Gon taught Obi-Wan. Dooku eventually left the Order entirely. Qui-Gon died still questioning. Obi-Wan became the most orthodox exemplar of what his master had quietly resisted.

## The Sith Code

### Text and Structure

> Peace is a lie. There is only Passion.
> Through Passion, I gain Strength.
> Through Strength, I gain Power.
> Through Power, I gain Victory.
> Through Victory my chains are Broken.
> The Force shall free me.

In some versions the opening line is repeated after the closing line. The Code is also called *Qotsisajak* in-universe (Wookieepedia "Code of the Sith," citing Greg Rucka's canon novel *Guardians of the Whills*). The text first appeared in canon in *The Clone Wars* episode "Altar of Mortis" (Season 3, 2011). David Gaider created it in 2003 for *Knights of the Old Republic* by reversing the Jedi Code and adding a philosophy Wookieepedia notes was "partly inspired by Mein Kampf." In Legends the Code is attributed to Sorzus Syn, one of the Dark Jedi exiled after the Hundred-Year Darkness, whose journals were compiled into the in-universe *Book of Sith* (Daniel Wallace, 2012). The Sorzus Syn attribution is Legends-only.

### Line by Line

**"Peace is a lie. There is only Passion."** The Sith's structural answer to the Jedi Code's first line. Where the Jedi negate emotion and affirm peace, the Sith negate peace as a lie and affirm passion exclusively. The Sith accusation is that the Jedi have constructed a false picture of reality to maintain their power. Passion, affect with direction and force, is the only real engine of action.

**"Through Passion, I gain Strength. Through Strength, I gain Power. Through Power, I gain Victory."** Three lines that form a causal chain. Passion produces strength, strength produces power, power produces victory. A Nietzschean argument for the will to power. Wikipedia's *Sith* article notes the parallel: "Sith value 'master' virtues, such as pride and power, whereas the Jedi value altruistic 'slave' virtues like kindness and compassion."

**"Through Victory my chains are Broken."** Darth Maul's partial recitation in *The Clone Wars* episode "Brothers": "Through victory my chains are broken. The chains. The chains are the easy part. It's what goes on in here that's hard." The Code promises external freedom. It cannot deliver internal peace. Maul knows the line and cannot feel it.

**"The Force shall free me."** The closing line shifts agency. Through the previous lines, the speaker has been the agent ("I gain"). Here the speaker becomes the patient. The Force, raw material throughout the Code, becomes the actor. The Sith conclude by claiming that the Force itself endorses and accomplishes their liberation.

### Inversion and Instability

The Code inverts the Jedi Code precisely at the first line and structurally throughout. The Jedi mantra is five parallel denials and affirmations describing a state of being. The Sith Code is a causal escalation describing a process of becoming. The Jedi rest in what is. The Sith are driven by what is not yet achieved.

Sith philosophy holds that conflict purifies and that strength deserves to destroy weakness. Applied externally this is a strategy. Applied internally it is a self-destruction mechanism. Every apprentice is incentivized by the Code to surpass and eventually destroy the master. Every master knows this. Wikipedia's *Sith* article: "The fate of Sith Lords is to be killed and replaced by their own apprentices."

The Rule of Two emerged from this collapse pattern. Darth Bane, observing the Brotherhood of Darkness consuming itself around 1,000 BBY, engineered the order's destruction at Ruusan and instituted the Rule: one master to embody power, one apprentice to crave it. Bane's backstory specifics are Legends. The Rule of Two itself is canon. Even refined, the Rule still depends on a relationship the Code guarantees will turn lethal. Once the Jedi are gone the Sith have only each other to conquer.

## Traditions of Balance

The Force is older than the Jedi Order. Several traditions, some canon and some Legends, frame it not as a contest between light and dark but as a polarity to be held in balance.

### Ashla and Bogan

In Legends material from *Dawn of the Jedi* (Dark Horse, John Ostrander and Jan Duursema, 2012), the planet Tython has two moons: bright Ashla and dark Bogan. The first Force-sensitive settlers named the two aspects of the Force after them. Tython itself enforced balance, answering with storms and quakes when the Force tipped toward either pole. The Je'daii Master Ketu: "Observing the world around them, they saw two moons in the one sky, light Ashla and dark Bogan, and they understood the dual aspects to the Force, light and dark. The light defined the dark as the dark did the light." The Bogan moon remains Legends. The term "Ashla" was canonized when the Bendu uses it in *Rebels*.

### The Je'daii Order

The Je'daii Order (Legends, c. 36,453-25,783 BBY) was a Force tradition on Tython founded by pilgrims gathered by the Tho Yor pyramid ships. Their name combines *je'* (mystic) and *dai* (center) from the language of the Dai Bendu monks. It means "mystic center." Je'daii who tipped too far toward the dark were exiled to Bogan to meditate on Ashla's light. Je'daii who tipped too far toward the light were exiled to Ashla to meditate on Bogan. Both extremes were punished equally. The Order fractured during the Force Wars (~25,793-25,783 BBY) when one faction embraced only Ashla and another only Bogan. The light faction won and refounded itself as the Jedi Order. The dark faction was destroyed. The Jedi inherited Tython's tradition by abandoning half of it.

### The Force Wielders and Mortis

The Mortis arc (*The Clone Wars* Season 3, 2011) is canon's most explicit metaphysical text. Mortis is "a conduit through which the entire Force of the universe flows," an ethereal realm inhabited by three beings, the Ones: the Father (balance), the Son (dark), the Daughter (light). The Father describes himself directly: "It is only here that I can control them. A family in balance. Day with night. Destruction, replaced by creation." When the Father dies and the Son and Daughter both fall, balance on Mortis is restored not through the victory of either side but through the dissolution of the system. This mirrors the Je'daii cosmology of millennia before and foreshadows the Bendu of millennia after.

### The Bendu

The Bendu (canon, *Star Wars Rebels* Season 3, 2016, voiced by Tom Baker) is canon's most explicit personification of Force neutrality. He defines himself to Kanan Jarrus: "Jedi and Sith wield the Ashla and Bogan. The light and the dark. I'm the one in the middle. The Bendu." The naming connects directly to the Dai Bendu monks of the Je'daii era. His neutrality is not passive. When Kanan calls him a coward in the Battle of Atollon, the Bendu rises into a storm and attacks both Rebel and Imperial forces. He prophesies Thrawn's defeat ("like many arms surrounding you in a cold embrace") and vanishes. Balance, in his form, cares nothing for either side's cause.

### The Guardians of the Whills

The Guardians of the Whills (canon, established by 483 BBY) are spiritual warrior-monks based at the Temple of the Kyber in Jedha City. They are not Jedi. They have no lightsabers. They are attuned to the Force but do not wield it overtly. Chirrut Imwe and Baze Malbus from *Rogue One* are the two appearances most viewers will know.

Their mantra, recited as walking meditation: "The Force is with me, and I am one with the Force; and I fear nothing, because all is as the Force wills it." Their Sunset Prayer: "In darkness, cold. In light, cold. The old sun brings no heat. But there is heat in breath and life. In life, there is the Force. In the Force, there is life. And the Force is eternal" (Greg Rucka, *Guardians of the Whills*, canon novel, 2017). The Guardians focus on the totality of the Force rather than the Jedi-Sith binary. When the Empire pillaged Jedha for kyber crystals, the Guardians could not stop them, and they remained anyway, keeping vigil at a fallen site.

### The Whills

The Whills are George Lucas's deepest cosmological layer. In his earliest drafts they were an immortal order recording the saga as the *Journal of the Whills*. Lucas dropped the framing and said directly: "the concepts behind the Whills turned into the Force" (*Star Wars: The Annotated Screenplays*). Before selling Lucasfilm he conceived them as microscopic life forms that fed off the Force, with midi-chlorians as their conduits: "The Whills, in a general sense, they are the Force" (*James Cameron's Story of Science Fiction*, AMC, 2018).

The *Journal of the Whills* is canon. Alan Dean Foster's *The Force Awakens* novelization opens with Verse 7:477:

> First comes the day / Then comes the night.
> After the darkness / Shines through the light.
> The difference, they say, / Is only made right
> By the resolving of gray / Through refined Jedi sight.

The phrase "the resolving of gray" is woven into the founding document of the canonical Star Wars universe.

### The Force Priestesses

Five ethereal beings reside at the Wellspring of Life, "the home of the midi-chlorians" (canon, *The Clone Wars* Season 6: "Voices," "Destiny," "Sacrifice"). They appear as five aspects: Serenity (the leader), Joy, Anger, Confusion, Sadness. They are "representative of the connection between the Living Force as experienced by living beings, and the Cosmic Force in its role in the greater scheme of the universe."

They taught Yoda the secret of retaining individual identity after death, the path Qui-Gon had pioneered. Serenity to Yoda: "At death, in order for you to preserve your identity, you must know yourself, your true self, and then let go." This is the paradox at the center of Jedi mastery. To keep the self the self must be released. The Sith refuse this release. That refusal is precisely what bars them from the path.

### Grey Jedi

In Legends, "Grey Jedi" had two senses: Force users who walked the line between light and dark without falling, and Jedi who distanced themselves from the Council and operated outside Code structures. True Grey Jedi met both criteria.

Jolee Bindo (Legends, *Knights of the Old Republic*, 2003) is the clearest example. A Padawan during the Old Sith Wars, he married a woman named Nayama against the spirit of Jedi teaching and watched her fall to the dark side. He could not kill his wife when they dueled. Disillusioned with the Council's response, he exiled himself to the Shadowlands of Kashyyyk for twenty years. He claimed to see "only in gray."

Qui-Gon Jinn was called a Grey Jedi by his contemporaries in Legends. Jedi Master Tyvokka in *Star Wars* #36 (Marvel, 1998): "Some think he is a gray Jedi." Canon describes him as a Living Force practitioner who "strove to follow the will of the Force even when his actions conflicted with the wishes of the High Council."

Ahsoka Tano is the most prominent canon character to live between the paths. Framed for a temple bombing, expelled, exonerated, she chose not to return. Her white lightsabers, per Dave Filoni, signal that she is "neither a Jedi nor a Sith." Her line to Vader in "Twilight of the Apprentice" (*Rebels* Season 2): "I am no Jedi." In E. K. Johnston's canon novel *Ahsoka* (2016) she lives under the alias "Ashla," the Je'daii name for the light, an intentional choice. She does not oppose Jedi values. She is alienated from the institution.

## Core Philosophical Tensions

### Attachment Versus Love

The most quoted canon dialogue on this comes from *Attack of the Clones*. Padme to Anakin: "Are you allowed to love? I thought that was forbidden for a Jedi." Anakin: "Attachment is forbidden. Possession is forbidden. Compassion, which I would define as unconditional love, is central to a Jedi's life. So you might say that we are encouraged to love."

The clarification comes from the character who will most dramatically fail to live by it. Wookieepedia gives the doctrinal definition: "Attachment was the inability to accept change as the fundamental characteristic of life; to accept death as the natural part of life; the inability to let go." The Code does not forbid feeling. It forbids the possessive structure built around feeling. Attachment is the act of trying to hold the world still. Compassion is the act of loving its motion.

### Living Force and Cosmic Force

Two complementary aspects of the same Force. The Living Force is the energy of all life, immediate and present, generated by every breathing being. The Cosmic Force is the wellspring from which the Living Force springs and into which it returns at death. The Force Priestess Serenity to Yoda: "When a living thing dies, all is renewed. Life passes away from the Living Force into the Cosmic Force and becomes one with it. One powers the other. One is renewed by the other."

Qui-Gon Jinn is the canonical Living Force practitioner. His instruction to Obi-Wan in *The Phantom Menace*: "Be mindful of the living Force." The Council favored the Cosmic Force, the long view, prophecy and destiny. Qui-Gon favored presence and immediate response. Both are correct. Neither alone is sufficient. The High Republic Master Obratuk Glii expresses the integration: "I cannot be sad that a loved one has become one with the greatest power in the galaxy, because they remain with us, always, through the Force. Nor can I fear the moment it happens to me."

### Action and Contemplation

Yoda to Luke in *The Empire Strikes Back*: "You will know [the good side from the bad] when you are calm, at peace, passive. A Jedi uses the Force for knowledge and defense, never for attack." The Jedi are encouraged to rely on instinct over analysis. Combat expertise comes from training that has become reflex. Qui-Gon's teaching: "Your focus determines your reality." The thinking has already happened. The discipline is the precondition for spontaneity.

### Fear, Anger, Hatred

Yoda's chain in *The Phantom Menace*: "Fear is the path to the dark side. Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate. Hate leads to suffering." This is not a list of bad moods. Fear is the gateway because fear is always fear *of losing something*. That structure is the structure of attachment itself. The chain is the unfolding of an original error.

The Wookieepedia "Dark Side of the Force" article gives the operative definition: "The dark side was greed, the fear of change, and the inability to let go." Even Yoda has a shadow. In *The Clone Wars* episode "Destiny" he confronts it: "Part of me you are, yes. But power over me you have not. Through patience and training, it is I who control you." The dark side is not absence. It is presence to be continually faced. The Sith do not stumble into this. They engineer it. The Rule of Two ensures permanent fear in both master and apprentice. Lucas, via Filoni: a character's intention, "their will to be selfless or selfish," is what separates light from dark.

### Compassion as Fulcrum

Lucas placed compassion at the center. "The light side is focused on compassion and giving; thus it is love, and the opposite of attachment, it is everlasting joy, devoid of fear of loss and the pain of loss." And: "As long as you love other people and treat them kindly, you won't be afraid." The Code formulation: "Notwithstanding the strictures against attachment, the Code emphasized compassion as an essential aspect to the Jedi way of life, teaching that all lives were precious." Prohibition and mandate are the same principle in two voices. The Jedi do not withhold love. They expand it past the individual object until it covers all life.

## Mastery: Being a Jedi Versus Doing Jedi Things

### The Trials

The traditional Trials of Knighthood, fully codified in Daniel Wallace's Legends reference *The Jedi Path* (2010) and treated as canonical structure in current continuity:

- **Trial of Skill.** Competence with the lightsaber and Force-enhancement of the body for combat. The only trial that tests external capability.
- **Trial of Courage.** Confronting one's greatest fears, often involving death or the failure that leads to another's death.
- **Trial of the Flesh.** Originally physical pain, later sacrifice and commitment. The candidate must choose between protecting the objects of their attachment and sacrificing themselves and those beings for the greater good.
- **Trial of the Spirit.** The candidate confronts their own shortcomings, often at a vergence in the Force, resisting emotional weakness against the darker aspects of their character.
- **Trial of Insight.** Resisting illusion and deception, often through a riddle clouded by further illusion.

Master Klefan Opus on a candidate who failed the Spirit trial: "She did not complete the Jedi trials. It was the Trial of the Spirit that got her. She lacked focus and concentration, couldn't control her mind. It was as if her spirit had broken" (canon novel *Red Blade*). The Lothal temple in *Rebels* tests a parallel sequence: teamwork, isolation, fear, anger, betrayal, focus, instinct, forgiveness, protection. The forms vary. The substance does not. Four of five canonical trials test the inner life.

### The Saber Before the Trials

The Gathering, the rite that produces the kyber crystal, happens before the Trials. The Initiate enters the Crystal Caves of Ilum, finds a crystal attuned to their Force presence after overcoming an inner obstacle, and constructs a saber under Professor Huyang's guidance. Only later, as a Padawan, do the Trials come.

The order matters. The saber is not a weapon the Padawan trained to use. It is an artifact of who they already are. The crystal that responds to them reveals something true about their relationship with the Force and themselves. The hilt they assemble gives that inner truth physical form. The Trials then test whether the self that built the saber has the moral foundation to wield it. The Trial of the Flesh tests whether attachment will override commitment. The Trial of the Spirit tests emotional weakness. The Trial of Insight tests the candidate's ability to see through illusion.

Mastery is not demonstrated by skill at the end. It is demonstrated by the coherence between the self that came through the Crystal Caves and the self that passes the Trials. The saber is made first because the saber is who the Jedi already is. The Trials measure whether that self can hold what it has built.

### The Heart of It

Luke Skywalker in *Secrets of the Jedi* (canon): "The Jedi Code itself may seem simple upon reading it, but the true challenge is living it. Passion can rise to the surface. Emotions can cloud our thoughts. Chaos can spiral us into the unfathomable darkness. We all find ourselves staring into the abyss at times. Only those who find a way to rise above the darkness are deemed worthy to become Jedi."

Qui-Gon Jinn in *Master and Apprentice* (Claudia Gray, canon, 2019) gives the fulcrum:

> "It matters which side we choose. Even if there will never be more light than darkness. Even if there can be no more joy in the galaxy than there is pain. For every action we undertake, for every word we speak, for every life we touch, it matters. I don't turn toward the light because it means someday I'll win some sort of cosmic game. I turn toward it because it is the light."

This is the answer to the Sith Code. The Sith promise that victory will free them. The Jedi say the choice is the freedom. The Jedi is not primarily a person who can do remarkable things with the Force. The Jedi is a person who has chosen the light, not for advantage and not for reward, but because they understand what the light is and why it matters. The lightsaber, built before the Trials, made from a crystal that answered the candidate's own truth, is the visible sign of that invisible choice.
